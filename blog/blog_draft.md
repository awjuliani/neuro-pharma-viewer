# Five Ways Drugs Can Change Synaptic Signaling

To say that the brain is complex is an understatement. It has an almost fractal-like level of intricacy, where each time neuroscientists think they’ve reached the most basic level, they find that there is a whole new level of complexity hiding within it. This has especially been the case in the domain of psychiatry and psychiatric drug development, where with each decade more is learned about just how many potential ways there are for a drug to interact with the information signaling process of the brain. A particularly striking example is our understanding of the role of serotonin in the brain. Serotonin was identified in the mid-twentieth century, but subsequent research revealed that it signals through multiple receptor families and subtypes with very different cellular effects. Even at a single receptor, different ligands can vary in efficacy, duration, and the signaling pathways they favor. 

As more is learned about the brain, it becomes possible to reason about new classes of drugs which might impact the brain in different ways, with different profiles of what their effects and side effects might look like. These drugs often center around how they impact the transmission of a signal between two or more different neurons. Like a radio signal, these transmissions convey a spatiotemporal pattern based on when and where signaling chemicals are sent across a synapse from a presynaptic neuron to a postsynaptic neuron. Different classes of psychiatric drugs impact this signal in different kinds of characteristic ways. Again returning to serotonin, both an antidepressant like escitalopram and the investigational PTSD drug MDMA impact serotonin signaling, but do so in drastically different ways. In order to better explain the intuition behind how this happens, I developed a simple interactive visual and auditory simulation of a toy synapse. If you would like to jump straight to interacting with the tool itself, [the link is here](https://awjuliani.github.io/neuro-pharma-viewer/). The rest of this post walks through what it can do and some of the intuition it can help convey.

*Disclaimer: The simulation is designed to convey the basic intuition about the pattern of signal transmission and how psychiatric drug classes impact it. Due to its various simplifications, it is not intended to accurately model a synapse or be used to guide any clinical decision making.* 

![][image1]  
The basic simulation visually and auditorily (be sure to unmute it) conveys a simple spatiotemporal pattern of signaling between a hypothetical presynaptic and postsynaptic neuron. The particles are released from the presynaptic neuron and diffuse across the synapse towards the postsynaptic neuron. There are receptors on the postsynaptic neuron with which the particles can interact. When a particle locks into a receptor, it sets off a reaction that conveys a signal within the neuron. Depending on the type of receptor and its role within the neuron, that signal may either contribute to the neuron becoming more likely to fire, less likely, or to do something else within it. Particles eventually detach from the receptor and may diffuse back across the synapse towards the presynaptic neuron. If this happens, the presynaptic neuron has transporters which can reuptake the particles back into the cell to be recycled. In a highly simplified way, this is the basic lifecycle of many neurotransmitters. In addition to the animation, I also included a music staff with notes to better visually convey the spatial and temporal nature of the signaling. 

A psychiatric drug has a number of different places where it might influence this process, and depending on which way it does so, the nature of the change to the dynamics of signaling will be different. Overall I have included five intervention mechanisms in the simulation: agonist, antagonist, releaser, reuptake inhibitor, and allosteric modulator. Below I walk through each, discussing their effects and provide an example of each.

An agonist will bind to the receptors on the postsynaptic cell, causing them to send their signal within the cell. Depending on their strength, they may force the receptor to continue signaling much longer than the endogenous transmitter would have, or may produce other changes in how the signal is communicated. In the simulated animation, this results in the signal being sent across the synapse becoming drowned out by the activation happening on the postsynaptic cell itself. Within the serotonin system, a well known example of an agonist is psilocin, the active metabolite of the psychedelic drug psilocybin. Psilocin binds to 5-HT2a serotonin receptors, and causes a cascade of effects which are vastly different from what endogenous serotonin is capable of.

![][image2]

On the other hand, a drug might bind to the receptors on the postsynaptic cell, but do so in a manner that blocks any transmissions from being communicated within the cell. These are referred to as antagonists, and their effect is to typically reduce signaling through that receptor population. Within the simulated animation, this results in the signal becoming more sparse and stripped down. A well known example is ketanserin, a 5-HT2a antagonist which is often used to block the effects of psychedelic drugs such as psilocybin. 

![][image3]

In the case of the releaser, the drug interacts with the transporter on the presynaptic cell, forcing it to release rather than reuptake the transmitter particle. Releasers such as MDMA interact with monoamine transporters and can promote transmitter efflux while also reducing reuptake. The animation represents this as an outward-releasing transporter, omitting the intracellular and vesicular steps involved. MDMA has been studied as part of MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD, although that use is not FDA-approved.

![][image4]

Rather than forcing additional transmitter to be released from the transporter, a reuptake inhibitor also impacts the transporter, but does so by preventing a transporter from clearing transmitter efficiently. Within the simulated animation, this has the effect of increasing the noise within a transmission, but does so in a manner that better preserves the pattern of the original signal than the releaser. Within the brain the story is more complicated due to various homeostatic mechanisms. The most well-known examples of this class of drug are the selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) such as escitalopram (Lexapro) and fluoxetine (Prozac). 

![][image5]

Finally, there are the allosteric modulators, which bind not to the receptor itself, but to sites on or nearby the receptor which modulate the signal that it sends within the neuron when activated. Within the simulated animation, I provide a representation of a positive allosteric modulator (PAM), which increases the strength of the signal without changing the temporal pattern of that signal. Real PAMs can also alter affinity, efficacy, and signaling kinetics. An example of a serotonergic PAM is Oleamide, though it remains a research chemical with relatively restricted use. Because the spatiotemporal pattern may remain more intact, a number of psychiatric drug developers are exploring PAMs as possible next-generation replacements for existing drugs which act in one of the other mechanisms. Of course, this class of drugs has its own downsides, and may still induce adaptation and tolerance, [suggesting there is not necessarily any free lunch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_free_lunch_theorem).

The simulator also has a number of different parameters that can be adjusted to see their impact in real time, such as changing the strength of the intervention or how long each drug binds to its target. I encourage people to try out different combinations to get a sense for how they change things, and in particular to how they change both the spatial and temporal nature of the signal being communicated. After playing with it a lot myself, I can say confidently that interacting directly with it provides a much better intuition than the static images provided in this post do.
