# Five Ways Drugs Can Change Synaptic Signaling

To say that the brain is complex is an understatement. It has an almost fractal-like level of intricacy. This has especially been the case in the domain of psychiatric drug development, where with each decade more is learned about just how many potential ways there are for a drug to interact with the information signaling process of the brain. Serotonin was identified in the mid-twentieth century, but subsequent research revealed that it signals through multiple receptor families and subtypes with very different cellular effects. Even at a single receptor, different ligands can vary in efficacy, duration, and the signaling pathways they favor. 

Psychiatric drugs often work by impacting the transmission of a signal between two or more different populations of neurons. Like a radio signal, these transmissions convey a spatiotemporal pattern based on when and where signaling chemicals are sent across a synapse from a presynaptic neuron to a postsynaptic neuron. Different classes of psychiatric drugs impact this signal in different and characteristic ways. Both the approved antidepressant drug escitalopram and the experimental drug psilocin impact serotonin signaling, but do so in drastically different ways. If you aren’t a neuropharmacologist, these effects can often get confusing. In order to better explain the intuition behind how the different mechanisms work, I developed a simple interactive visual and auditory simulation of a toy synapse. If you would like to jump straight to interacting with the tool itself, [the link is here](https://awjuliani.github.io/neuro-pharma-viewer/). The rest of this post walks through what it can do and some of the intuition it can help convey.

*Disclaimer: This is a qualitative, not-to-scale teaching tool. Its particle counts, paths, controls, and signal markers are visual parameters, not biological measurements, doses, or predictions of clinical effects.*

The basic simulation visually and auditorily (be sure to unmute it\!) conveys a simple spatiotemporal pattern of signaling between a cartoon *presynaptic* and *postsynaptic* neuron. The particles are released from the presynaptic neuron and diffuse across the synapse towards the postsynaptic neuron. There are *receptors* on the postsynaptic neuron with which the particles can interact. When a particle locks into a receptor, it sets off a reaction that conveys a signal within the neuron. Depending on the type of receptor and its role within the neuron, that signal may either contribute to the neuron becoming more likely to fire, less likely, or to do something else within it. Particles eventually detach from the receptor and may diffuse back across the synapse towards the presynaptic neuron. If this happens, the presynaptic neuron has *transporters* which can reuptake the particles back into the cell to be recycled. In a highly simplified way, this is the basic lifecycle of many neurotransmitters. Below the main animation, there is a music staff with notes to better visually convey the spatial and temporal nature of the signaling. 

There are a number of different places where a psychiatric drug might influence this process, and depending on which way it does so, the nature of the change to the dynamics of signaling will be different. The simulation includes five intervention mechanisms: agonism, antagonism, transmitter release, reuptake inhibition, and positive allosteric modulation. Below, I describe what each mode depicts and give a representative example.

**Agonist**: An agonist directly binds to a receptor and stabilizes an active signaling state. In the animation, agonist particles activate receptors independently of presynaptic transmitter pulses. Increasing the visual binding-duration control makes those displayed activations last longer, allowing them to dominate the musical staff. Psilocin, the active metabolite of the psychedelic drug psilocybin, is a serotonergic agonist whose psychedelic effects depend strongly on 5-HT2a receptor activation, although it interacts with multiple serotonin-receptor subtypes.

![][image2]  

**Antagonist**: On the other hand, a drug might bind to the receptors on the postsynaptic cell, but do so in a manner that does not transmit a signal within the cell. These are referred to as antagonists, and when they bind to a receptor, they prevent endogenous neurotransmitters from being able to bind, thus reducing overall signalling. Within the simulated animation, this results in the overall pattern represented on the musical staff becoming more sparse and stripped down. A well known example within the serotonin domain is ketanserin, a 5-HT2a antagonist which is commonly used in research to attenuate 5-HT2a mediated psychedelic effects of drugs such as psilocin.

![][image3]

**Releaser**: Drugs can also impact the presynaptic cell to impact overall signalling pattern. Releasers interact with monoamine transporters on the presynaptic cell and can promote transmitter efflux while also reducing reuptake. The animation represents this as an outward-releasing transporter, omitting the intracellular and vesicular steps involved. A well known example in the serotonin domain is MDMA, a compound capable of inducing the efflux of serotonin from transporters that is currently under investigation for use as an adjunct to therapy in the treatment of PTSD.

![][image4]

**Reuptake Inhibitor**: Rather than forcing additional transmitter to be released from the transporter, a reuptake inhibitor also impacts the transporter, but does so by preventing a transporter from clearing transmitter efficiently. Within the animation, reuptake inhibition creates additional receptor events after a release pulse while leaving the original pulse pattern visible. The releaser mode adds events independently of those pulses, making the displayed pattern appear less tightly organized around presynaptic release. Within the brain the story is more complicated due to various homeostatic mechanisms. The most well-known examples of this class of drug are the selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) such as escitalopram (Lexapro) and fluoxetine (Prozac). 

![][image5]

**Allosteric Modulator**: Finally, there are the allosteric modulators, which bind to a regulatory site on the receptor that is distinct from the transmitter’s orthosteric binding site. This modulates the signal the receptor sends within the cell when it is activated at the orthosteric binding site. Within the simulated animation, I provide a representation of a positive allosteric modulator (PAM), which increases the strength of the signal without changing the temporal pattern of that signal. Real PAMs can also alter affinity, efficacy, and signaling kinetics. An example of a serotonergic PAM is Oleamide, though it remains a research chemical with relatively restricted use. PAMs are of interest in drug development because they can enhance responses to endogenous transmitter and may sometimes provide greater receptor-subtype or context dependence. That does not automatically make them safer: their effects and liabilities remain receptor- and compound-specific.

The simulator also includes adjustable animation parameters, such as displayed intervention abundance and visual binding duration. These are not doses, concentrations, or measured molecular residence times. I encourage readers to experiment with them and observe how they change the displayed spatial and temporal pattern.
